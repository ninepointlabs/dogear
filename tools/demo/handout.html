Unit 3: Photosynthesis

Biology 101 · Reading for week 6

How plants make food

Every leaf is a small factory. Using only sunlight, water from the soil and carbon dioxide from the air, a plant builds the sugar it lives on, and releases oxygen as it goes. This process, photosynthesis, feeds almost every food chain on Earth: the energy in the bread you ate this morning was sunlight a few months ago.

Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts, small green structures packed into the cells of a leaf. Their color comes from chlorophyll, a pigment that absorbs red and blue light and reflects green. That reflected green light is the color we see when we look at a forest.

The overall reaction. Six molecules of carbon dioxide and six of water, with the energy of light, become one molecule of glucose and six of oxygen: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂.

The reaction runs in two stages. The light reactions capture energy from sunlight. The Calvin cycle then spends that energy to build sugar from carbon dioxide. The two stages take place in different parts of the chloroplast, and they depend on each other: neither can keep going for long without the other.

Why does any of this matter outside a biology class? Because the oxygen in every breath you take was released by photosynthesis, and the fossil fuels we burn are the remains of plants that captured sunlight millions of years ago.

The light reactions

The light reactions take place in the thylakoids, stacks of flattened sacs inside the chloroplast. When chlorophyll absorbs light, the energy excites electrons, which pass along a chain of proteins in the thylakoid membrane, like a bucket brigade.

To replace the electrons it loses, the plant splits water molecules. This is where the oxygen comes from: it is a by-product of splitting water, not of carbon dioxide, a fact first shown with labeled oxygen atoms in the 1940s.

The energy of the moving electrons is stored in two carrier molecules, ATP and NADPH. These carry the energy to the second stage. Think of them as charged batteries that the Calvin cycle will use up.

Remember. Light reactions: in the thylakoids; need light and water; produce ATP, NADPH and oxygen.

The light reactions stop when it gets dark, and so, soon after, does the Calvin cycle, because it runs out of ATP and NADPH.

The Calvin cycle

The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma, the fluid that surrounds the thylakoids. Here the plant does the actual building. It takes carbon dioxide from the air and attaches it to a five-carbon sugar already in the cycle, a step called carbon fixation.

The enzyme that does this is rubisco, probably the most abundant protein on Earth. It is also slow and easily confused: about one time in four it grabs oxygen instead of carbon dioxide, which wastes energy. Plants make up for its slowness by making a lot of it.

Using the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, the cycle turns the fixed carbon into a three-carbon sugar. Some of it leaves the cycle to become glucose, starch and cellulose; the rest regenerates the five-carbon sugar so the cycle can turn again.

Remember. Calvin cycle: in the stroma; needs carbon dioxide, ATP and NADPH; produces sugar.

What limits photosynthesis

A plant photosynthesizes only as fast as its scarcest ingredient allows. On a dim morning light is the limit; on a bright day it may be carbon dioxide or temperature instead.

Temperature matters because the Calvin cycle is run by enzymes. Too cold and they work slowly; too hot and they lose their shape. Water matters too: when a plant is short of water it closes the pores in its leaves, which keeps water in but carbon dioxide out.

Some plants have found ways around rubisco's mistakes. Corn and sugarcane concentrate carbon dioxide before the Calvin cycle sees it. Cacti open their pores only at night, storing carbon dioxide until the sun comes up.
